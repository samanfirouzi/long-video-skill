# Cinematography options

A pool to draw from. Suggest 3 to 4 per group, chosen for the story. Adapt each to the locked art style and the config's visual approach.

Two tracks:

- **Real camera language** for live-action looks, realistic AI images and AI video.
- **Virtual camera language** for illustrated, 2D, stickman and still-image looks. The camera is simulated by moving, scaling or layering images. Use these words for those styles.

## 1. Camera and format look

Real camera:
- Cinema digital camera, clean and sharp, shallow depth of field
- 35 mm film look, grain, soft highlights, warm blacks
- 16 mm documentary look, rough grain, natural color
- Handheld documentary camera, natural and close
- Vintage video look (VHS or early digital), soft and noisy
- Drone and aerial footage for scale
- Locked-off tripod, formal, symmetrical frames
- Found-footage or body-camera feel

Virtual camera:
- Locked frame with the art doing the work, like a picture book spread
- Multiplane parallax: layers move at different speeds for depth
- Animated-feature camera: smooth virtual dolly and crane moves through a painted world
- Comic-panel feel: frames cut like panels, with held poses
- Stop-motion feel: limited frame rate, tactile movement

## 2. Lenses and framing

- Wide (24 mm equivalent): place, scale, isolation
- Normal (35 to 50 mm): natural, honest, conversational
- Portrait (85 mm): faces, intimacy, soft background
- Telephoto (135 mm and up): compression, tension, surveillance feel
- Macro: objects and small details
- Anamorphic: wide cinematic frame, oval blur, horizontal flares
- Symmetrical center framing: control, formality, unease
- Rule-of-thirds framing: natural storytelling
- Low angle: power. High angle: vulnerability. Overhead: pattern and map view
- Dutch angle (tilted horizon): used sparingly for unease

Shot sizes used in the script: extreme wide (EWS), wide (WS), medium (MS), medium close-up (MCU), close-up (CU), extreme close-up (ECU), over-the-shoulder (OTS), point of view (POV), insert (a cutaway to an object).

## 3. Camera movement

Real camera:
- Static: calm, observational
- Slow push-in: builds focus or emotion
- Slow pull-out: reveals context, isolation
- Pan (left or right): follows or reveals
- Tilt (up or down): shows height or scale
- Dolly or tracking: moves with a character or along a space
- Handheld: urgency, realism
- Crane or jib: rise or fall, big reveals
- Orbit (circle around a subject): importance
- Whip pan: fast transition, energy
- Rack focus: shifts attention between subjects

Virtual camera (for still images and 2D):
- Slow zoom in or out on the image
- Pan across a wide image
- Parallax drift between layers
- Push through a layered scene
- Ken Burns style: slow zoom plus pan on a still
- Shake or jitter for impact (short)
- Cut to a detail crop (an insert from the same image)

Say honestly what each production method can do. Still images with a moving virtual camera are cheap and reliable. True character action needs animation or video generation.

## 4. Lighting

- Natural daylight, soft: honest, documentary
- High-key (bright, few shadows): friendly, light, clear
- Low-key (dark, strong shadows): drama, mystery, tension
- Chiaroscuro (strong light and dark contrast): painterly drama
- Golden hour (warm, low sun): nostalgia, warmth, closure
- Blue hour or twilight: calm, melancholy
- Overcast flat light: sober, serious, realistic
- Practical lights (lamps, candles, screens in the scene): intimate, realistic
- Neon and mixed color light: urban, modern, cyberpunk
- Backlight or silhouette: mystery, reverence
- Hard top light: interrogation, harshness
- Fog and haze with light beams: atmosphere

Name a default lighting for the film, and optional variants by scene type (for example: default soft daylight, flashbacks in warm golden light, danger scenes in low-key).

## 5. Color and grade

- Natural and neutral
- Warm and faded (nostalgia)
- Cool and desaturated (tension, modern, sober)
- Teal and orange (cinematic contrast)
- High contrast black and white with one accent color
- Pastel and soft (gentle, young audiences)
- Saturated and bold (energetic, playful)
- Monochrome per chapter (each act has its own color)

Always keep the palette inside the locked art style. Quote hex codes from the style block when one fits.

## 6. Pacing and transitions

Average shot length (ASL) is limited by the clip length rule: no shot longer than 10 seconds, most shots 5 to 8 seconds. Create pace through shot size, movement and cut rhythm inside that range.
- Slow and contemplative: 7 to 8 seconds, with slow moves and dissolves
- Steady storytelling: 6 to 7 seconds
- Brisk: 5 to 6 seconds, with more cuts on action
- Rare quick inserts: 2 to 4 seconds, only for impact, never as the main rhythm

Transitions:
- Hard cut (default)
- Slow cross-dissolve (time passing, memory)
- Fade to black (chapter end)
- Match cut (shapes or movement link two shots)
- Whip or swipe (energy)
- Smash cut (shock)
- Sound bridge (audio from the next scene begins early)

For long videos suggest a rhythm over time, for example slow opening, steady middle, brisk build, slow ending.

## Choosing well

- Match the mood of the story, then the audience and tone from the config.
- Keep the film style small and consistent: one camera look, a short list of lenses, a short list of movements, one default lighting. A narrow palette of choices looks professional. A random mix looks chaotic.
- Match production reality. Do not promise what the user's tools cannot produce.
- Keep a reason for every choice, tied to the story.
